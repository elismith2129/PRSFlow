Chapter 01

You own the building

PRSFlo is two worlds. Billing is the coordinator's — invoices, payments, money owed. Operations is yours — four studios running around the clock, the runners who keep them running, and the paperwork that proves every day went right. You're also the person the operation reports to, so the last two chapters of this manual are about overseeing the other roles, not doing their jobs.

The three questions your screens answer

Your day runs on three surfaces, and each one answers a different question. The Dashboard — what's happening today? Daily Ops — did yesterday go right? My Day — what do I owe today? Keeping the three questions separate is deliberate: a screen that tries to answer all of them answers none of them well.

The habit to buildMorning order: Dashboard for Flo's briefing → Daily Ops until the queue is empty → My Day check-ins as you go. When Daily Ops says "Yesterday is done," it is.

What's yours

The daily duties (checklists, mic inventory, petty cash, stock), flags and their resolutions, studio tasks, shift logs, runner timecards, the morning review, and the studio check-ins. Nothing about money — sessions become invoices in the coordinator's world, already covered.

What's deliberately not

Work orders and invoices live in Billing (you oversee — chapter 10). Punch corrections live in HR. Today's live status is the dashboard's job. One copy of everything, one home for each thing.

Chapter 02 · The flow

A day, start to finish

The billing manual follows a session from hold to paid. Yours follows a day — from the moment a runner walks in to the moment you clear the last queue item the next morning. Every screen you use is a window onto this cycle.

1
You · ahead of the shift

The day is set up

Sessions sit on the calendar (each already carrying its work order). Anything you need done at a studio goes up as a studio task — scoped to a studio and a shift, never a person, because runners rotate. Whoever opens Encore next sees Encore's tasks first thing.

Studio + shift, never a name
2
Runners · their phones

Runners arrive and work the hub

A runner opens the hub, lands on their studio, and sees: tasks waiting, today's sessions, and the duty tiles. Through the shift they work the work order day sheet for each session (times, staff, equipment, payments — billing's chapter covers what happens to it) and the studio's own duties: opening and closing checklists, mic inventory, petty cash, stock.

3
Runners · as things happen

Problems become flags, not memories

A ripped couch, a dead mic, a leak — the runner marks needs attention on a checklist or work order (note + photo), and PRSFlo raises a flag automatically. Anyone can also raise one by hand. A flag is a record from the moment it exists — it will be waiting for you in the morning, and it cannot be forgotten because it never lived in anyone's memory.

4
Runners · through the shift

The shift log is written as it happens

The shift log replaced the Slack post. It's a log, not a text box: entries added through the shift, each stamped with who and when, photos welcome, handoffs mid-shift supported — and append-only. An entry is a record, not a draft; nothing is ever edited or deleted.

5
Runners · same day, protected

Punches

Hours live in ADP, not PRSFlo. What PRSFlo holds is the missed-punch report — the written confirmation California requires before a punch is edited, filed by the runner the same day.?Same-day reports are the ones that protect the runner — that's why the punch report sits on the hub itself, not buried in a menu. Your end of it is the ADP timecards duty on your My Day card: clear the exceptions, tick the duty, type how many.

6
You · next morning

The morning review — Daily Ops

Open Daily Ops. The left column is the queue — everything about yesterday that needs a decision, worst first. The right side is the sweep — all four studios at a glance. Work the queue to zero, skim the sweep, and the page tells you the finish line exists: "Yesterday is done."

Queue — decideSweep — verifyYesterday is done
7
You · out of the morning

What leaves the review

A flag worth acting on gets acknowledged and becomes work — a vendor call, a task, a conversation. A missing submission becomes a question for today's runner. Your studio check-ins (chapter 07) get ticked as you go. And the work orders from yesterday? Already in the billing hub, being reviewed — not your queue.

The whole cycle in one line

tasks posted → runners work the day (duties · flags · shift log · punches) → morning queue to zero → sweep read → "Yesterday is done" → set up today

Absence is the loudest signal

A checklist that came in with a problem announces itself. A checklist that never came in used to be the quietest thing in the system — nothing rendered, nowhere. It's now a hot queue item, above almost everything else, because the state that most needs your attention is the one with no pixels of its own.

Why clearing existsThe queue isn't a to-do list — it's a "seen" list. Clearing an item says a manager looked at this and dealt with it or chose not to. That's what makes "Yesterday is done" mean something.

What's deliberately not on this page

No work orders (Billing reviews those — chapter 10 is your window into it), no punches (HR), no live today (the dashboard). The one exception to clearing: a flag clears only by acknowledging the flag itself — the flag system stays the permanent record, this page is just the door.

Chapter 04

Flags

A flag is how a problem in the building becomes a record instead of a memory. Anything broken, missing, leaking, or wrong gets one — raised automatically from a runner's needs-attention note, or by hand by anyone.

The life of a flag

Pending — just raised Acknowledged — you've seen it, it's being handled Resolved — fixed, with the story attached

Pending flags sit in your Daily Ops queue and on the dashboard's flags panel. Acknowledging says "a manager knows" — you can recategorize it at the same moment. Resolving closes it with a resolution note, and when money was involved, the vendor and the cost — so a year from now "when did we last fix that AC and what did it cost" has an answer.

Every flag carries its comment thread and photos. Runner-raised flags keep the runner's original note — that's the primary source; don't retype it.

Door vs filing cabinetThe Daily Ops queue is where flags meet you in the morning. The /flags page is the standing record — every flag ever, searchable, by category. Don't work from the cabinet; work from the door.
Chapter 05

Studio tasks & shift logs

Two tools for talking to a shift you won't be awake for.

Studio tasks — work you leave for the building

Scoped to a studio and a shift, never a person — runners rotate, so "whoever opens Ameraycan" is the only assignee that always exists. Tasks sit at the top of the runner hub, above the sessions, because a runner's first question is "is anything waiting for me?" You post them, runners complete them, done ones collapse away.

Don't confuse them with My Tasks on the dashboard — those are personal, assigned by name, for office staff. Studio tasks belong to a room and a time of day.

Shift logs — the day, written down

One log per studio per day, entries appended as things happen, each stamped with author and time. Multiple runners can write into the same day — that's a handoff, on the record. Nothing is ever edited or deleted.

You read them two ways: the sweep card shows a preview of each studio's day, and the full log opens in a popup. A good log answers tomorrow's questions before they're asked — that's the standard to hold runners to.

The grey sub-linesA check-in stands for four small things (opener/closer, sessions, Slack, the check-in itself) — the grey text keeps them visible, but there's one tick per duty. The tick means all of it happened.

The rules the card lives by

Day-scoped duties show on their day. Office stock appears Wednesday; valley checks are the exception — shown every day so you can do them whenever, but due Tue and Fri, and a skipped week still goes red.

Missed days accrue on cumulative duties. Skip timecards two days and one line reads "covering 3 days" — one tick clears the backlog it names.

Never "late" on the day itself — late means a previous day went unfinished. And anyone can cover: another admin can work your card when you're out; the record shows who did what.

This is the exact box from your dashboard — the slow ring of light around it is Flo's signature. Glow means AI: nothing else in the app glows, so if something is glowing, it's Flo.

Reading it, line by line

Red — slippedA backlog hit 3+ days, a monthly duty blew its date, or yesterday's duty went unticked. The whole line turns red and bold — that's the one visual alarm Flo has.
Amber — buildingPressure in the system, counted fresh each morning. Some ambers are billing-world numbers; they're context for you, work for the coordinator.
Green — went wellEarned, never decorative — only when every duty due yesterday was done.
TomorrowThe day-before nudge for weekly/monthly duties. Never repeats something already red.

What clearing means

Red clearsby doing the duty — one tick covers the backlog it names. No dismiss, no snooze.
Amber clearsitself when reality improves — the queue behind it empties and the line is gone next morning.
Green & Tomorrowaren't clearable — a report and a heads-up.

Chapter 10 · Oversight

Billing, from where you sit

The coordinator reports to you. This chapter is not how to do billing — that's the Billing manual, one click away, and you can read it any time. This is how to know the job is being done, and when to walk over.

What the coordinator owes, at a glance

Daily: Ramp approvals + receipts · review yesterday's work orders · update yesterday's invoices in QuickBooks · COD outstanding follow-up. Mondays: the Ramp weekly report and the sent-invoice follow-up pass. Monthly: tenant rent — invoiced the 25th, chased the 5th.

You don't track this by asking — the staff grid on the dashboard shows billing's row going green each day, and their misses show in Flo exactly like yours do.

Your four numbers — the billing peek

Your My Day page carries a read-only box built precisely for this. Four numbers, no actions:

Why read-onlyEli's ruling: you oversee the billing period, but your world shouldn't be cluttered with billing work. Four numbers is oversight; anything more is doing the job.

When to walk over

COD "Balance due" above zero — money that should have been collected at the desk wasn't. Rare and critical; it should never sit more than a morning.

The 31+ number growing — sent invoices aging past a month. One is a slow client; a trend is a chasing problem.

"In progress" piling up morning after morning — yesterday's work orders aren't being reviewed same-day. The whole flow rests on that morning habit.

COD outstanding stuck at "—" — the counts aren't being typed, which means the follow-up duty isn't really being worked, and Flo is flying blind on it.

How to read it

The hub remembers its studio, greets by time of day (the studios run 24/7 — "tonight" at 7 AM would be wrong), and stacks the shift in your priority order: tasks first (your voice on their screen), then sessions — each opening its work order day sheet — then the duty tiles, then the shift log. The punch report and manual sit quietly at the bottom.

What runners can and can't do

Can: everything that happens at the desk — times, staff names, OT that follows from the clock, equipment condition, payments taken, rentals, notes, flags. Can't: rates, schedule dates, the client block, approved days, or any admin page — and they see only the Runner manual, not this one.

The one rule they're taught about submissions: you can fix your day until the office approves it. If a runner is nervous about editing a submitted day — that's the answer.

Chapter 12

Help & words

When something looks wrong

A studio's day looks empty — before assuming the worst, check who worked: the sweep card names everyone who touched that day. Empty AND staffed is the real signal.

A red "NOT saved" message — the save failed and the app said so honestly. Retry; if it persists, tell Eli — errors are logged.

The manual doesn't match the screen — the app changed and the manual didn't. Say so; the version history (bottom of the rail) lists what changed per release.

Words this manual uses

Queue — yesterday's exceptions, worst first. Sweep — the four studio cards. Clearing — marking a queue item seen; shared across managers. · Flag — a problem as a record; pending → acknowledged → resolved. · Studio task — work scoped to a studio + shift, never a person. · Shift log — the day's append-only record, per studio. · Punch report — the same-day missed-punch confirmation (legal record). · Duty / backlog — a recurring My Day item / its missed prior days. · Check-in — your daily per-studio duty with its sub-items. · The peek — your four read-only billing numbers. · Flo — the briefing; computed, rule-based, no AI.
