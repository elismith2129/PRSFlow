Three things we changed in the old ADP instructions

1. Final pay timing

The prior instructions said final pay was due within 72 hours of termination. We've rewritten it as immediate at time of termination for involuntary separations (LC 201); last day worked for a quit with 72+ hours notice; within 72 hours for a quit with shorter notice (LC 202) — accrued unused PTO included in all three.

Because involuntary means the check has to exist before the conversation, PRG-P02 now requires 48 hours' notice to Eli and Lynair before any planned involuntary termination.

2. CalSavers

The onboarding steps said to email Lynair to remove the employee from CalSavers — the termination action, in the wrong section. It's an add at hire, a remove at separation.

Corrected further after review: enrolment can't happen until onboarding is complete, since it needs the employee's address and SSN. So the trigger is onboarding completion, and Fernando emails Lynair the moment it closes. The 30-day statutory clock still runs from the hire date, which means a slow onboarding compresses the window rather than extending it.

3. Hire date convention

Setting the ADP hire date seven days before the start date gets the onboarding invite out in time for paperwork to finish before day one. Confirmed as the standing convention.

Worth applying uniformly rather than case by case — the hire date also drives payroll eligibility, benefits eligibility, and the CalSavers window above.

How the systems divide up

ADP Workforce Now remains the system of record for pay. Every punch correction is entered in ADP by hand. There is no integration and that's deliberate — a sync that silently drifts from ADP would be worse than no sync.

PRSFlo is the platform we use internally for operations. Its role in these procedures is narrow: it collects what staff report, timestamps it, and holds the record. It never writes to ADP. If the two ever disagree, ADP is right about what was paid and PRSFlo is right about what was requested and confirmed.

The screens below are mockups, not built software. Where a feature isn't finished, each procedure carries the manual fallback — so nothing waits on the build.

Procedure 1 — Daily timecards

Daily review by 11 AM rather than a scramble in the 48 hours before payroll. The reasoning is simple: reconstructing a punch from three days ago is guesswork, reconstructing yesterday's is a two-minute conversation. It's the same total work, moved to where it's cheap and accurate.

The rule everything hangs on

No punch is edited without written confirmation from the employee. Not because we distrust anyone — because a corrected timecard with no employee sign-off behind it is unverifiable, and a manager editing time with no confirmation looks, on paper, exactly like a manager shaving hours.

So the confirmation has to be captured somewhere durable. The mechanism is a form on the employee's phone:

Employee timecard approval

Added at Lynair's suggestion, and it strengthens the whole procedure. Every employee approves their own timecard in ADP each period — compliance was good at WFN rollout and has slipped since.

An approved timecard is the employee confirming their own hours. A text confirms one punch; an approved timecard affirms every hour in the period, in the system of record, with a timestamp. It's the single most useful document we could produce in a dispute.

Fernando chases outstanding approvals during the daily review rather than at period end — a same-day nudge is easy, a two-week-old one is a chore. A manager never approves on an employee's behalf; that produces something that looks like employee confirmation and isn't, which is worse than no approval. And a refusal to approve is useful information, since it usually means the employee disputes the hours — better surfaced before payroll runs.

Closing the period

The handoff to Lynair used to be implicit — Fernando did what he could, Lynair found what was left, and neither knew where the boundary sat. It's now explicit and binary. Fernando doesn't hand over a period until seven things are true: every day reviewed, zero open exceptions, every employee timecard approved, every edit commented with confirmation on file, meal premiums listed, PTO and sick entered, and the punch log current.

Until the software is ready

Staff text or email instead of submitting in the app, and those messages get saved — they are the written confirmation and there's no substitute. Misses are logged by hand and ADP comments typed manually. The procedure is identical; only the storage changes.

Procedure 2 — Missed punches

Staff forget to punch. Sessions run long, a client wants one more pass, and the clock-out never happens. The aim isn't to eliminate that — it's to move responsibility for a missed punch back to the person who missed it, at the moment they missed it.

Two rules that don't bend

All hours worked are always paid. A missed punch never delays or reduces pay, regardless of how many times someone has forgotten.

Pay and discipline are separate tracks. Never dock pay, withhold a check, delay a check, or cut hours over a missed punch. Accountability is a performance conversation and nothing else.

The second is where well-meaning managers get companies sued. Docking pay feels proportionate and reads as an unlawful deduction from wages — turning a coaching issue into a wage claim, and making every prior correction look suspect. It's stated this bluntly in the procedure on purpose.

The mechanism

The whole design rests on one distinction: a miss the employee reports, versus a miss the manager discovers.

How it surfacedEffect
Employee reports it in writing, same dayLogged, corrected, no conversation, no count
Employee reports it lateHalf a miss — two late reports equal one counted
Manager finds it in the daily reviewCounted — enters the coaching ladder

Staff work out that arithmetic within a pay period or two. And because the submission is timestamped, the classification is computed rather than judged — no memory, no argument, and no tally for Fernando to maintain. The behaviour we want isn't "never forget a punch," which is unrealistic here. It's "own your own time record." Someone who forgets four punches a month and reports all four the same day is a success.

The ladder

Counted misses only, rolling 90 days, reset by a clean 90.

CountAction
1stQuick word in person, 60 seconds. Re-state the standard.
2ndSame conversation plus a written recap emailed to the employee, copied to Eli.
3rdDocumented meeting with Eli. Written expectation, 30-day check-in.
4th+Formal performance action per the handbook.

It escalates on pattern, not incident. A formal track starting at the first miss would be disproportionate and would kill the self-reporting incentive outright — if the first miss produces a warning, nobody volunteers anything and the system inverts.

Separately, and not on this ladder: buddy punching, reporting hours not worked, refusing to confirm hours, and misses that cluster around overtime or meal-break thresholds. Those go straight to Eli.

Procedure 3 — Hiring and separation

This is checklist work with hard legal deadlines attached, which makes it a bad fit for memory. The design intent is that deadlines are dated automatically off the start date or last day worked, rather than remembered.

Eli creates the record in ADP — a name, mobile, email, start date, plus position and rate. Because he enters the rate himself, nobody needs to review it afterwards. Fernando runs everything around it, including reviewing and approving the new hire's uploaded documents.

Separation — one question first

The separation flow asks how the employment is ending before anything else, because that single answer sets the final pay deadline and nothing downstream is correct without it.

Separation — how is this ending?
We are terminating — fired or laid offpay due immediately
They quit, 72+ hours noticepay on last day
They quit, under 72 hours noticepay within 72 hrs
Final check must be ready before the termination conversation. Includes all accrued unused PTO. Requires Eli's approval and 48 hours' notice to Lynair. Cannot proceed until both are recorded.
This is the highest-dollar item in the whole set — waiting time penalties under LC 203 run up to 30 days of wages, and they apply even when the delay was an honest administrative miss. So it's built as a block rather than a reminder. The rest of the separation checklist — separation packet, ADP steps, CalSavers removal, final timecard, access and equipment, personnel file — only opens once that gate is satisfied.

One further rule: a voluntary resignation is never processed on a verbal notice. Written resignation is required, both because a verbal one is ambiguous and because the date and time notice was given is what sets the deadline above.

Harassment training — January 1, 2027

All staff trained by January 1, 2027. We're working to an internal December 1 date for a month of buffer. Roughly 15 employees, so we're covered by the mandate.

Plan: build the roster and classify everyone supervisory or non-supervisory in August, assign in September, chase through November. Anyone with no record on file is treated as untrained.

We're proposing the free CRD courses rather than a paid vendor for this cycle — compliant and manageable to track at our size. Happy to use a vendor instead if you'd prefer, particularly one that bundles the annual workplace violence training.

On classification, we've read supervisory as anyone who can hire, assign, discipline, or direct another person's work — owners, managers, assistant managers, and anyone directing runners, techs, or engineers. Where it's unclear we default to supervisory and the two-hour course. We'll use ADP's Manager classification as the first pass on the roster, then check it against that definition, since the legal test is broader than an org chart.

We're not attempting to run this through ADP. Its training workflow amounts to reminders inside a convoluted process built for a much larger audience — the same experience as the performance review process. The obligation here is a fifteen-row list with two date columns, and the risk is losing track of a due date, not lacking a sophisticated system. Keeping it simple.
